# CW-024 Posture/Framing Research (report only — nothing built)

Nick's question, verbatim scope: the roadmap response was delivered as
"defeat + homework" ("prove seasonal projects with real orders") instead of
help; should picking an option ever return a "your whole plan fails" wall;
is the roadmap-to-fundability firing at the wrong moment in the wrong tone.

Evidence base: Cedar Ridge draft 7deeafb2 messages [108]–[118] (the actual
turns), the roadmap/opening/converged copy in section.py, the milestone
builder in controller.py, and the corrected re-run (payroll 225k lands →
share 77.8% vs the 70% wall; the corner no longer collapses on a phantom).

## 1. What actually happened at the posture moment

Turn [117] was the client's most cooperative act of the whole walk — "Let's
go with option 2 — trim direct costs only." The reply at [118] was the
roadmap: "I have to be straight with you… even that best case comes up
short… So instead of a plan that says you fail…" followed by four
milestones phrased as homework. Three separate wounds in one message:

1. **The pick itself was never acknowledged.** No "that landed, direct
   costs are now X, the gap moved to Y." The client's chosen lever
   disappears into a terminal verdict. (Contrast [116], which DID open
   with "That moved the plan - the gap just closed by $1,951" — the
   acknowledgment shape already exists and reads well.)
2. **The verdict was factually wrong** — built on the $361k phantom. The
   slate fixes this class: with corrections landing, the corner is
   computed on the client's real numbers. On the corrected shape, Cedar
   is not roadmap territory at all; it is a walking file with one honest
   wall (payroll share 77.8% vs 70%, revenue-to-clear $321,429).
3. **The homework was upside down.** "Payroll staged to revenue (the
   believable team floor is $260,000/yr)" was said to a client whose real
   team costs $225,000 — the app's own judged floor exceeded their actual
   payroll, because the floor was fitted to the phantom. Every milestone
   inherited the corruption.

Posture finding #0, therefore: **most of the perceived cruelty was a data
integrity failure, not a copy failure.** The defeat was wrong, so its
tone had no chance. The slate (doors, group-row prevention, retention
consumer) removes the class of roadmap that fires on numbers the client
has already corrected. What remains below is the posture question for
TRUE roadmap cases — files where the honest corner really does fail.

## 2. Should picking an option ever return "your whole plan fails"?

Structurally, what happened: the option landing re-evaluates, and the
re-evaluation can flip status to roadmap in the same turn. So the client's
"yes" is answered with "it's over." Research answer: the flip may be
computed in that turn, but it should never be *delivered* as the direct
response to a pick. Two-beat shape (for ruling):

- **Beat 1 (always):** the receipt for the pick — what moved, by how
  much, where the gap stands. The client's action is honored first,
  factually. This machinery exists ([116] proves it).
- **Beat 2 (only then):** if the corner has genuinely collapsed, the
  honest exit — as its own turn, or as a clearly separated close after
  the receipt, never replacing it.

A pick that WIDENS the gap already has honest handling (the "something is
off, tell me which figure looks wrong" shape). The roadmap flip deserves
the same courtesy: the arithmetic first, the conclusion second.

## 3. Is the roadmap firing at the wrong moment?

The corner-first doctrine (roadmap when even the most favorable realistic
configuration fails) is sound and Nick has ruled it repeatedly: no plan
ships that fails on paper. The timing problem observed is narrower:

- At Cedar it fired **mid-walk, immediately after a pick**, because the
  landing's re-evaluation collapsed the corner. The client experienced it
  as punishment for cooperating.
- The corner had been passing at gate entry ([108] says "a version that
  works exists"). When a mid-walk landing collapses the corner, that is
  a *signal about the landing or the data*, not only about the business —
  at Cedar it was the phantom. A corner that flips mid-walk on a
  client-accepted lever move is anomalous enough to deserve a
  consistency check (did a lever landing REDUCE the corner? that should
  be near-impossible for an accepted option) before the defeat ships.

Research recommendation (for ruling, not built): treat a corner flip
inside a walk as a tripwire — hold the roadmap, disclose "this last
change made the numbers worse, which usually means an input is off; here
is what changed," and only enter roadmap once the client confirms the
inputs. Roadmap at GATE ENTRY (corner fails before any walk) keeps its
current immediate timing.

## 4. Tone of the roadmap itself ("defeat + homework")

The current message leads with the strongest negative frame available
("I have to be straight with you… comes up short… a plan that says you
fail") and then assigns milestones phrased as proofs the client owes
("prove X with real orders"). What the client cannot hear at that moment
is that this is the SAME consultant who just spent an hour helping.

Reframing directions (copy-level, one message, no machinery):

- **Lead with what is true and strong**: the business as described runs X
  in revenue with a real team and real clients; the plan that clears a
  lender needs Y; the distance is Z. Distance framing, not failure
  framing. ("Comes up short" → "here's the distance between today's shape
  and a fundable one.")
- **Milestones as the plan, not homework**: each milestone already has a
  number attached (up to $45,000/quarter at 55% margin). Phrase them as
  "the fastest paths to close the distance," ordered by dollar impact,
  with the client's existing strengths named as the vehicle ("your
  existing contract clients are the shortest path — a seasonal
  enhancement pilot with two of them would…").
- **Keep the client's agency**: "which of these is closest to something
  you're already doing?" is an invitation; "prove it with real orders" is
  an exam. Same milestone, opposite posture.
- **Keep the two hard promises** (already good, keep verbatim): numbers
  stay saved and rerunnable; nothing ships pretending, nothing fakes.
- The "That's not a judgment of you" line protests too much — a message
  that must say it isn't a judgment reads as one. The distance framing
  makes the disclaimer unnecessary.
